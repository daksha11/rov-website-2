Most first sessions go badly for one reason, and it is not nerves or talent. It is that the artist did not know what the hour was for, so they spent it deciding instead of recording.

Here is what actually happens, what to bring, and where the time goes.

## Before you book

**Have the song finished.** Not perfect, finished. Lyrics written, structure decided, key settled. The studio is the most expensive place in the world to write, and every room in Atlanta charges for the hour whether you are recording or thinking.

**Know your key and your tempo.** If you are recording to a beat, have the file, know the BPM, and have it in a format that is not a phone voice memo. If you do not know the key, say so at the start rather than an hour in.

**Rehearse it out loud.** Not in your head. Sing or rap the whole thing at performance volume at least a few times. Things you never noticed silently, a line you cannot get a breath before, a run that sits at the top of your range, show up immediately when you actually perform it.

**Bring reference tracks.** Two or three songs that sound like what you want this to sound like. Not songs you like, songs that sound like the target. References end arguments in five minutes that adjectives cannot end in an hour.

## What actually happens in the room

**Setup, roughly 10 to 20 minutes.** The engineer sets the mic, sets your headphone mix, and gets your level right. You will be asked to talk or sing at normal volume while they adjust gain. This is not wasted time, it is the part that decides whether the mix is easy or painful later.

Your headphone mix matters more than people expect. If you cannot hear yourself properly you will push, go sharp, or drift off time. Say something. "Can I get more of me" or "less beat" is a normal request and takes ten seconds.

**Tracking, most of the session.** You will record the same part several times. This is normal and it is not a comment on your ability. Engineers take multiple passes so they can pick the best line from each, which is called comping.

Expect to do the hook more times than the verses. Hooks carry the record and they get the most attention.

**Punching in.** Rather than restarting from the top every time, the engineer will drop you in for a specific line. Listen for the count and come in at performance energy, not at "I am just fixing a line" energy. Mismatched energy between a punch and the take around it is one of the most obvious problems to hear later and one of the hardest to fix.

**Rough mix.** Many sessions end with a quick rough so you can hear roughly what it will sound like. A rough is not the mix. It is a snapshot.

## The mistakes that cost the most

**Recording too hot.** Peaks should land around -12dB, not slammed to the top. A hot recording bakes in distortion that cannot be removed. Your engineer will handle this, but if you are self-recording, this one costs more records than anything else.

**Standing too close to the mic.** Six inches, with a pop filter. Closer is not better; it builds up low end that has to be carved back out.

**Trying to fix a performance with takes.** If the tenth take is not landing, the problem is usually not the take. Move on, come back to it, or change the line. Sixteen attempts at one bar is an hour gone.

**Booking too little time.** A single song from setup to usable takes is realistically two to three hours for a first session. One hour is enough to set up and record a hook you will probably redo.

**Not backing anything up.** Ask for your session files or stems at the end. A studio should give them to you.

## After the session

**Do not judge it that night.** You are too close to it and you have heard the hook ninety times. Listen the next day, on earbuds and in a car.

**Send notes once, specifically.** "The vocal feels buried in the second verse" is actionable. "It sounds off" is not. Grouping your notes into one message rather than five gets a faster and better revision.

**Sort your splits before release.** In writing, while everyone is still happy. Producer, features, writers. This is the step people skip and the only one that gets more expensive with time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should I bring to my first recording session?

The finished song with lyrics settled, your beat as a proper audio file with the BPM, two or three reference tracks that sound like your target, water, and headphones you are used to if you have them. Most importantly, arrive with the arrangement decided so the paid hour goes on recording rather than writing.

### How long does it take to record one song?

Realistically two to three hours for a first session, including setup and multiple takes. One hour is usually enough to get set up and track a hook you will want to redo. Experienced artists working on a familiar beat can move much faster.

### How much does studio time cost in Atlanta?

Atlanta rooms average around $102 an hour on rental marketplaces, with budget listings closer to $35 to $55. Ours is $65 an hour with mix and master included, or $200 for a four hour block. Always ask whether mixing is included in the hourly rate, because at most studios it is billed separately.

### Do I need to be able to sing in tune to record?

No, and pitch correction is a normal part of nearly every modern vocal chain. But tuning works best on a take that was already close and delivered with the right energy. Performance and timing are much harder to fix after the fact than pitch is.

### Should I record standing up or sitting down?

Standing, almost always. It opens your diaphragm and gives you more breath support and more energy. Sit only if the part genuinely calls for a quieter, closer delivery.

### What is comping in a recording session?

Comping is assembling one final vocal from the best moments of several takes. It is why the engineer records the same part multiple times, and it is standard practice on essentially every commercial record.

### Can I get my session files afterward?

You should be able to, and it is worth confirming before booking. Having your own stems means you are never locked to one studio for revisions or a future remix.
